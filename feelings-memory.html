She May Forget the Visit, but Not the Feeling

Why the feelings from a visit can outlast the memory of it, and what that means for caregivers.

The MindCadence Team 7 min read · Research explained

You spend an afternoon with a family member who has Alzheimer’s disease. You talk, share a pot of tea, sit together in the sun. The next day you call, and she says, “You haven’t come to see me in so long.”

Many families and caregivers have quietly asked themselves the same question: if she won’t remember, does my visit still matter?

It is a heavy question. Behind it there is often exhaustion, and a sense of loss. But the answer neuroscience offers may be gentler than we expect.

Remembering, and feeling

We tend to speak of “memory” as if it were one thing. In fact, the brain keeps more than one kind.

One kind is remembering what happened: who came by, what was said, what we ate. This kind of memory depends heavily on a structure deep in the brain called the hippocampus, which is among the first regions damaged by Alzheimer’s disease. That is why the most recent events are often the first to slip away.

Another kind is what we felt: joy, sadness, comfort, fear. Emotions arise and linger through other regions and networks of the brain. They work closely with the hippocampus, but they do not depend on it entirely.

Can these two come apart? Someone noticed more than a century ago.

A pin hidden in a handshake

In 1911, the Swiss physician Édouard Claparède had a patient with severe amnesia. Every time she saw him, it was as if they were meeting for the first time.

One day, as he shook her hand, he hid a pin between his fingers, and she felt a small prick. The next day she still did not recognize him, and could not say what had happened. Yet when he held out his hand, she would not take it.

She had forgotten the day before. The unease had stayed.

The story has been told in neuroscience for more than a hundred years. But it was a single case, and hard to test rigorously. It took another century before researchers designed experiments to examine “what we remember” and “what we feel” side by side.

Two experiments

The first: patients with amnesia (2010)

Researchers at the University of Iowa invited five patients with severe amnesia caused by damage to the hippocampus. On one day the patients watched about 20 minutes of sad film clips; on another day, happy ones. They reacted as anyone would: tears during the sad scenes, laughter during the funny ones.

About ten minutes after the clips ended, the researchers asked them what they had seen. A person with normal memory can recall around thirty details. These patients could recall almost nothing; one could not recall a single detail. Yet when asked how they felt right then, they still felt sad, or happy.

The feeling outlasted the memory of what caused it, by a long way.

The study was published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences (PNAS).1

The second: people with Alzheimer’s disease (2014)

The same team then asked: would the same be true for people with Alzheimer’s?

They invited 17 people in the early stages of the disease and 17 healthy older adults of similar age and sex, and repeated the experiment. About five minutes after the films, the patients remembered little. Four could not describe anything from the clips, and one did not remember watching any films at all. The healthy participants remembered far more and did much better on every memory test. Yet while watching, the two groups felt sadness and happiness with about the same intensity. In other words, the patients could not hold on to what had happened, but their capacity to feel was intact.2

And for the patients, the sadness or happiness lasted for up to half an hour.

One finding is especially moving: the less people remembered, the longer their sadness lasted.

Overall, sadness also tended to linger a little longer than happiness.

Put simply, forgetting what made us sad does not make the sadness go away. A person may just feel uneasy or low, without knowing why.

What these studies can and cannot tell us

To be clear, both are small laboratory studies: one with five patients, the other with seventeen. The emotions were brought on by film clips in a lab, and followed for only about half an hour. All the participants were in the early stages of illness; later in the disease, things may be different. And a visit at home is not the same as a film in a laboratory.

What they do show is that, in these patients, feelings could last longer than memories. What they cannot yet tell us is how long such feelings last in everyday life, or how much they shape a person’s wellbeing over time.

One more distinction is easy to blur: “feelings linger” is not the same as “emotion helps memory.” Healthy people tend to remember emotional events better, but in Alzheimer’s disease, studies disagree on whether emotion gives memory much of a boost.3 This article is about the first idea: the event is forgotten, but the mood it created remains.

Nor is any of this a treatment. It cannot slow the disease. But it can change the way we keep someone company.

What this means for caregivers

If feelings really can outlast memories, many small things we do take on a different weight.

First, a visit is never wasted. She may not remember that you came, but the comfort and pleasure of that afternoon may stay with her for a while. A phone call, a hug: they do not simply vanish because they are forgotten.

Second, tone and patience matter. When someone gets things wrong or asks the same question a third time, it is natural to correct them, even to argue. She will soon forget what the argument was about, but the hurt and unease may last longer. The researchers describe one patient who could not remember what happened at a medical appointment, yet disliked that doctor from then on.

Third, create more happy moments. The researchers’ suggestions are simple: visit often, talk together, move a little, listen to music, dance, share a joke, or cook a favorite meal. Music is especially remarkable, and our newsletter on November 15 will be devoted to it.

Fourth, be kind to yourself, too. Caregiving is a long road. No one can be gentle and patient every moment. If you lose your patience now and then, don’t be too hard on yourself. A caregiver’s feelings deserve care as well, and that is what our next article will explore.

In closing

Memories may slowly drift away, but the ability to feel warmth may last longer than we think.

So the next time you sit beside her, there is no need to ask whether she will remember. She may forget what you said, and still carry the feeling you gave her.
