Last Night, I Got Lost in a Dream

When the brain becomes its own screenwriter

Lindai A personal essay · 7 min read

Illustration created with AI

Last night I had a dream.

When I woke up, it struck me as so curious that I grabbed my phone and recorded it as a voice memo. I knew that if I didn't capture it right away, the scenes that were still so vivid a moment ago would soon vanish without a trace, like morning mist.

Here is the dream.

I was riding a bicycle to a remote place, a stretch of endless sand and dunes. Why I went there, I can no longer remember. There, I ran into Y, a classmate from college.

Later, we got ready to leave. Y was on a bicycle too. “Are you really going back?” she asked. “Yes,” I said. So she rode ahead, as if leading the way, and I followed. But she rode faster and faster, faster and faster… After a bend, I came to a fork where three roads met, and she was gone. And I cried.

A grown woman on a bicycle, crying her heart out at an unfamiliar fork in the road because she had lost track of a college classmate. Thinking about it after I woke up, it seemed a little funny. But in the dream, the hurt and helplessness were completely real.

Then someone told me, “Y's parents are just up ahead. Go to their house.” So I went.

Y's parents were very kind, and there were two little boys in the house. One of them, about six or seven, was crying, and the reason, of all things, was that he couldn't make sense of the pitches in his sheet music.

“7 is bigger than 1, so why do I have to sing 7 lower than 1?”

It turned out he was looking at a 7 with a small dot beneath it, a low 7. (In jianpu, the numbered musical notation widely used in China, the notes of the scale are written 1 through 7, and a dot below a number lowers it by an octave.) I crouched down and patiently began explaining math and music to him. I told him that numbers can be negative too, and that the little dot is like putting the 7 “below zero.” Negative 7 is smaller than negative 1, so you can't just look at how big the number is; you also have to see which level it sits on.

He seemed to understand, and stopped crying.

Looking back, there I was, lost in a strange place myself, solemnly explaining to a child what is high and what is low.

Lesson over, I still decided to head back to the city. I said goodbye to Y's parents, got back on my bicycle, and rode through a stretch of pitch darkness. There were no lights anywhere, and I had no idea where the road led. At last, far off to my left, I saw a blaze of lights. The place was bustling, and many people were singing.

It suddenly occurred to me that this was probably where I had lost my backpack earlier. When I had lost it, I had no memory at all. But in the dream, I simply knew that I had lost a bag and had to get it back.

Y was there too. She saw me, came over, and asked, “You're still leaving?” “Yes,” I said. So she took me to find my bag. We found it, and my laptop was still inside, but my clothes were gone. “Where are my clothes?” I asked her. “There was an earthquake. Your clothes were donated to the victims,” she said. I was startled. If my clothes were gone, what would I do when I got cold?

Y seemed to read my mind. She gave me a look that carried a hint of reproach: people have lost everything in a disaster, and you're still worried about your clothes?

I didn't ask again. Luckily, there seemed to be one piece of clothing left in the bag.

Strangely, I never asked where the earthquake had happened, or why no one had asked me first. In the dream, it all seemed perfectly natural.

Next, Y took me to rent a car. This time it wasn't a bicycle but something that looked like a race car. This kind is better, she said.

Just as I was about to leave, Y said to me, “I'm never leaving this place. I don't even need my phone anymore. It's nice here.” She said it very calmly, as if she had already made an important decision.

And I still had my heart set on going back to the city.

That was where the dream ended.

I woke up.

Lying in bed and turning the dream over, the more I thought about it, the more incredible it seemed. Desolate dunes, an old classmate, a little boy in tears over a musical scale, an earthquake who knows where, an inexplicable race car… How did all these unrelated people and events get stitched together?

My college classmate vanished, and I cried. A child didn't understand the scale, so I taught him negative numbers. My clothes went to disaster victims, and I only worried about being cold. I came on a bicycle but had to rent a race car to go back. Every bit of it felt perfectly reasonable.

It was as if my brain were writing the story while casting me in it, and I, the lead, had no idea what the next scene would bring.

This reminded me of a fascinating question in neuroscience: what is the brain actually doing when we dream?

In the past, people often saw dreams as mysterious omens, or as symbols of something hidden deep within. Modern neuroscience tells us that sleep is not a time when the brain stops working.

Especially during rapid eye movement (REM) sleep, many areas of the brain remain highly active. Regions involved in vision, emotion and memory take part, while some prefrontal areas responsible for cognitive control and reality-checking behave differently than they do when we are awake.

This may partly explain why dreams are so often full of intense emotion and vivid images, yet don't quite follow the logic of the real world.

Still, dreams don't happen only during REM sleep. Scientists are still studying how dreams arise, and how exactly they relate to memory processing and emotional regulation.

One more question makes me especially curious. Does the story in a dream really unfold in sequence? Or, after we wake, does the brain gather scattered fragments as it recalls them and arrange them into what looks like a complete story? After all, memory has never been a faithful video recorder.

Every time we recall something, we may reorganize it without realizing it. For dreams, which slip away so quickly, this seems especially worth pondering.

Who knows?

But one thing is certain: while I slept, my brain did not rest. In a world with no real stage, it created characters, scenes, dialogue, even tears for me.

And after waking, I could still remember some of it.

What lingers most is Y's last remark. She spoke so calmly, while I was set on leaving. There was no argument and no pleading to stay; we each simply made our choice. I don't want to explain it. It's just that her calm has stayed with me.

Thinking of this, I feel a little lucky that I picked up my phone that morning and recorded the dream in time. Otherwise, the little boy crying over a musical scale, the earthquake that happened who knows where, and Y's calm farewell might all have long since faded into the depths of memory.

By day, I use my brain to make sense of the world. By night, my brain used me to tell a story.
