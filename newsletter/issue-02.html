It’s not just music. It’s their music.

A brain-imaging study of favorite songs in dementia — and why to gather the music of a life while you can.

Download this issue (PDF) Two-page spread (landscape PDF)

① The Discovery
Observational study

A favorite song still reaches the brain

What they found. Researchers in Amsterdam scanned the brains of 60 people — 22 with Alzheimer’s disease, 13 with a form of frontotemporal dementia (FTD), and 25 healthy adults — as they heard their own favorite music and unfamiliar pieces. Favorite music stirred more brain activity. In Alzheimer’s, the response resembled that of healthy adults; in FTD, it was weaker.

Why it matters. Music is widely used in dementia care, mostly based on what families observe. This study looks inside the brain, and hints that the type of dementia matters.

What it means for you. This was a small, one-time scan; it does not show that music slows the disease or helps memory. But it echoes what many families sense: a song a person loves may still land. If music upsets them or no longer interests them, follow their lead.

van ’t Hooft JJ, et al. (2026). Music processing in behavioural variant frontotemporal dementia and Alzheimer’s disease: a functional MRI study. Brain Communications, 8(3), fcag196. Read the original study →

② The Human Side

A playlist of a life

“It’s not just music, it’s your music,” one of the researchers said. The songs that reach a person are rarely the ones we would choose for them.

So ask, while you can. What played at their wedding? What did they sing to their children, or dance to at twenty? Ask a sister, an old friend, the neighbor who remembers. Write the answers down, find the recordings, keep them in one place.

A playlist like this is a small archive of a life — and, someday, a way back into it.
