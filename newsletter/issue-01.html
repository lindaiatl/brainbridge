Slowing Alzheimer’s, not stopping it

For our first issue: a landmark trial — and why a familiar song can still find its way home.

Download this issue (PDF) Two-page spread (landscape PDF)

Music: “Peaceful Light Morning” by AudioCoffee, via Pixabay

① The Discovery
Clinical trial

Clearing amyloid modestly slowed early Alzheimer’s

What they found. In a clinical trial, 1,795 people with early Alzheimer’s disease (mild memory problems or mild dementia, with amyloid confirmed in the brain) received lecanemab — an antibody that helps clear amyloid — or a placebo every two weeks for 18 months. On a standard scale of memory, thinking and daily function, people on lecanemab declined about 27% more slowly.

Why it matters. Published in 2023, it was among the first trials to show that clearing amyloid can modestly slow the disease itself — groundwork for the treatments in use today.

What it means for you. Lecanemab slows decline; it does not stop or reverse it. About one in eight participants had brain swelling on scans, usually without symptoms, so careful monitoring is required. It is only for early-stage disease, and it is a decision to make with a specialist.

van Dyck CH, et al. (2023). Lecanemab in Early Alzheimer’s Disease. New England Journal of Medicine, 388(1), 9–21. Read the original study →

② The Human Side

The song that stays

Many caregivers notice that a song can reach a person when words no longer do. A melody from a wedding, a hymn from childhood, a tune once hummed while cooking. Memory may grow quiet in some rooms of the mind, while others stay lit for a long time.

If you care for someone, try the music of their twenties. Sing badly. Sing together. You are not testing their memory; you are keeping them company inside it.
