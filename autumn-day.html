The Long Autumn Day

Meeting another kind of American life in Tennessee

Lindai A personal essay · 5 min read

Illustration created with AI

When the air turns cool and yellow leaves appear on the ground, I know autumn has arrived. So we picked a day and drove to the Great Smoky Mountains to see the fall colors.

From Atlanta, the drive takes a little over four hours. The woods along the highway had already put on their autumn clothes, which made me look forward even more to the colors of the Smokies. As it turned out, the most beautiful scenery of the trip was on the road from Gatlinburg to Townsend.

The road isn't long, just over half an hour by car. It winds through the valley, and a clear creek runs sometimes on the right, sometimes on the left. The trees on the cliffs shimmered in the sunlight, their many-colored leaves glittering like gems in a kaleidoscope.

In that moment, my heart was filled with gratitude for this gift of nature.

Yet the bigger surprise came on the way home.

Hoping to buy fresh vegetables and peanuts, and to enjoy a freshly baked apple pie, we looked up a farmers market online, left the highway, and headed there. The market was quite remote. The last stretch was a dirt road, and a pale yellow cloud of dust rose behind our wheels.

When we arrived, the scene caught me by surprise. A woman in a long blue dress and a traditional bonnet, dressed like a country woman from old Europe, was pushing a stroller and waved to us. On another lane, to the clip-clop of hooves, a horse-drawn flatbed wagon rolled slowly toward us. Two men stood on it, guiding the horses.

I guessed they might be Amish, or perhaps Old Order Mennonites. These people still keep certain traditional ways of life, and they made a striking contrast with the modern world so close at hand.

Inside, the abundance of produce was a delight: fresh leafy greens, peppers of every shape and color, potatoes and sweet potatoes, and jars of pickles and chili oil lined up row after row. We felt as if we had stumbled into a treasure cave. Soon our cart was full, and we headed to check out.

The cashier was a handsome young man with an unusual little goatee. When he smiled, he showed slightly uneven teeth. He was chatting with a couple, and the conversation was a lively one.

We pushed our cart aside and waited. We weren't in a hurry, and there were no other customers, so why interrupt?

The woman was quite talkative. Her family kept three cows, she said, and milked them every morning at eight. They wanted three more, but their land wasn't big enough for six.

Oh, they had fresh milk! With vegetables this fresh, the milk must be good too. I couldn't help chiming in: "Do you sell your fresh milk?"

"No," she said with a smile. "We don't have that much yet. Hopefully when we have more, we can sell it here at the store."

I let out a soft sigh of disappointment. Looking around, I saw no fresh apple pie either. Still, we stood by and listened as they talked about cows, land, and life. Eventually another couple lined up behind us, and the three ahead of us began to wrap up their conversation.

As they parted, they introduced themselves by name.

Oh, so they didn't know each other! In this remote country market, complete strangers could talk about their lives with such ease.

Perhaps the land here is so vast and the people so few that every chance encounter feels precious. Or perhaps we simply happened to meet a few warm, sincere people. Either way, the easy trust between them warmed my heart.

After paying, we left the market with our arms full. The car rolled back over the dirt road, and once again a pale yellow cloud of dust rose behind the wheels. The autumn colors of the Smokies were far behind us now, yet the woods along the road still basked in the sunlight, their red, yellow, and orange leaves swaying in the breeze.

On the drive home, I kept thinking about that afternoon: gazing at the mountains, the water, and the trees full of autumn leaves; slowing down and standing to one side, listening to strangers talk about cows, land, and life. These were the most ordinary of things, yet they left me feeling wonderfully at ease. People often say that time in nature and connection with others nourish the brain. Perhaps that nourishment is hidden in ordinary days like this one.

A long autumn day, with mountains and rivers stretching into the distance; an autumn day lived slowly, simmering in the earthy fragrance of the land.
